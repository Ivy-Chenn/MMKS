    Hardware Systems \& Operating Principles

    LUBA 3 Board Link Diagram

    Figure 1. LUBA 3 Board Link Diagram.

    This chapter explains how the robot's power supply, charging system, motor drives, feedback signals and safety hardware work together. The connection diagram identifies physical components and wiring; the sections below explain their functional relationships. Circuit details, test points and limits must be checked against the service documentation for the relevant model.

    1 Hardware Architecture

    1.1 How the Systems Work Together

    A motor needs more than a start command. It needs a suitable power supply, a functioning driver, valid operating permission and a mechanical assembly that can move. The controller also needs reliable feedback to confirm that the requested action has occurred.

    System Role in Operation
    Power and battery Supply the electronics and motor drives through the relevant power paths.
    Charging Transfer power from the charging station into the battery under controlled conditions.
    Control Set the requested action and coordinate execution.
    Motor drive and feedback
    		 Apply controlled power to the motor and report its operating state.
    Safety hardware Provide safety inputs and, depending on the design, inhibit or interrupt hazardous outputs.

    These functions may share a board or be distributed across several boards. A board name does not define the full boundary of a system: a motor drive includes its power connection, control interface, output stage, motor wiring and feedback path.

    1.2 What a Hardware Symptom Tells Us

    A symptom identifies the function that failed, rather than the component that caused it. A wheel that does not turn may have a supply problem, an inhibited driver, a faulty connection, unreliable feedback or a mechanical obstruction.

    When several functions fail together, look for a shared supply, controller or connection. When one function fails, inspect its local path first. These observations help prioritize checks; they do not establish the root cause on their own.

    2 Power and Battery System

    2.1 Battery Output and Power Distribution

    The battery supplies both the robot electronics and the motor drives. Power distribution and voltage conversion provide the conditions required by each load, while switches and protection circuits determine whether a path is enabled.

    A successful startup confirms that the electronics receive enough power to start. It does not confirm that every supply branch is healthy or that the battery can support a motor load. A weak battery or a high-resistance connector may appear normal at rest but cause a voltage drop when a motor starts, resulting in reduced output, module resets or shutdown.

    2.2 Battery Management and Protection

    The battery management system (BMS) monitors and protects the battery according to its design, using information such as cell voltage, current and temperature. It can restrict charging or discharging when operating conditions exceed permitted limits.

    An interrupted power supply can therefore be caused by battery protection rather than a damaged driveboard. Check battery condition, output under load, connectors and protection status together. The displayed charge level alone is insufficient to establish battery health or available output capability.

    3.1 Docking and Charging Are Different Processes

    Docking places the robot at the charging station. Charging requires a continuous electrical path from the adapter, through the station and mating contacts, to the robot's charging circuit and battery.

    The charging circuit controls the electrical conditions applied to the battery. Battery state and protection conditions can prevent charging even when external power is available. Successful docking therefore does not prove that contact is established or that charging has started.

    3.2 Contact Quality and Installation

    Charging depends on stable contact alignment and pressure. Dirt, oxidation, worn contacts, uneven ground or a tilted station can cause intermittent contact or increased resistance. Increased resistance can produce voltage drop and heating when charging current flows, even if contact appears present.

    For a robot that docks but does not charge, distinguish three questions: does the station provide the required output, does that output reach the robot reliably, and does the battery accept charging? Check the adapter and station, contact condition and alignment, internal wiring and charging circuit, then battery state and protection reasons. Use the model-specific connection diagram to locate each part of this path.

    4.1 Controller Driver and Motor

    The controller specifies the requested direction, speed or position. The driver switches power to the motor to produce the required torque and movement. Feedback allows the controller to compare the actual state with the target and adjust output or stop operation.

    The control signal and motor power supply are separate requirements. A valid command cannot produce movement if the driver lacks power or is disabled. Conversely, a powered driver does not guarantee that it has received a valid command or reliable feedback.

    4.2 Motor Functions and Mechanical Load

    Motor Function Required Result Relevant Checks
    Wheel drive Produce the requested wheel movement. Drive output, motor feedback, wheel resistance and ground interaction.
    Cutting Maintain cutter rotation under the cutting load. Grass load, entanglement, obstruction, mechanical resistance and current.
    Height adjustment Move the mechanism to the requested height. Drive output, transmission movement and position or state feedback.

    Higher mechanical load generally requires greater torque and current. Dense grass, entanglement, a blocked disc or a tight bearing can therefore trigger overcurrent or overload protection without the motor itself being damaged. If the fault persists under normal low-load conditions, investigate the drive stage, motor, wiring and current-sensing path as well as the mechanics.

    4.3 Feedback and Hall Signals

    Feedback provides evidence of motor rotation, speed or mechanism position. In designs that use motor Hall sensors, their signals can support rotor-position detection, commutation and speed measurement. The exact use depends on the drive design.

    A motor may be mechanically capable of turning while its feedback is unreliable. A Hall fault can originate in the sensor, its supply, a connector, the feedback wiring or the receiving circuit. Persistent faults require hardware inspection because reliable operation cannot be assumed from visible movement alone.

    Current feedback serves a different purpose: it helps regulate torque and detect excessive electrical or mechanical load. Interpret current together with movement and operating conditions. A command with little movement and high current suggests a different failure path from a command with no drive output.

    5 Safety Hardware

    5.1 Safety Inputs and Output Inhibition

    Safety hardware converts a physical condition into an electrical state. An emergency-stop mechanism, safety key or lift-related sensor may use contacts or a sensor to provide that state. Depending on the design, it is evaluated by the controller, a dedicated safety circuit or both.

    The resulting action may inhibit the drive stage, request a controlled stop or interrupt a relevant power path. A stop command and an inhibited power stage are different mechanisms, although both can prevent movement. The required response can also differ between wheel and cutter drives.

    5.2 Safety Key and Drive Permission

    The safety key provides an operating interlock. Removing it can prevent hazardous outputs while leaving the main electronics powered. The robot may therefore remain available for status checks even though motor operation is prohibited.

    Possible implementations include a controller-detected key input, a hardware signal that disables the driver, or switching devices that interrupt a relevant circuit. The connection diagram alone cannot establish which implementation is used. Inserting the key satisfies one condition; it does not override other safety or protection conditions.

    If key removal makes the wheels easier to turn, the driver may have released active holding or electrical braking and entered a high-impedance, or coasting, state. This can be achieved by switching off the power-stage MOSFETs without physically disconnecting the motor wires. The reduced resistance does not, by itself, identify whether the change was commanded by software or enforced by hardware.

    5.3 Emergency Stop and Safety Sensors

    An emergency stop provides a direct physical input for stopping or preventing hazardous operation. Other safety-related inputs report conditions such as lifting or displacement of a monitored mechanism. Their response is defined by the product's safety design.

    When operation is inhibited, compare the physical condition with the state reported by the system. A mismatch may indicate a faulty contact or sensor, incorrect alignment, a wiring problem or an input-circuit fault. A correctly detected unsafe condition is a valid reason for stopping, rather than evidence of hardware damage.

    5.4 Hardware Protection

    Overcurrent, abnormal voltage and excessive temperature can also restrict output to protect the battery, driver or motor. Determine what triggered protection before deciding which part to replace.

    Removing the safety key is not proof that the entire robot is electrically isolated. Follow the model-specific shutdown and isolation procedure before servicing, and do not bypass safety inputs during troubleshooting.

    Applying the System View

    For any hardware fault, identify the requested action, the power path, the drive or charging output, the relevant feedback and the safety conditions. Verify the suspected link under controlled conditions, change one factor at a time and repeat the original operating scenario after repair. This connects the observed symptom to a tested cause instead of assuming that the component named in the error is defective.
